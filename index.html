School in Exile

“Education is the key to our survival.” — H.H. the Dalai Lama

My new life began in Mussoorie, at the Tibetan Homes School. It wasn’t just a place to learn reading or math—it was where I learned what it meant to be Tibetan. Every morning started with the national anthem, not of the country I lived in, but of the homeland I had left behind. We celebrated Losar, read Tibetan poetry, and listened to the teachings of His Holiness the Dalai Lama.

This school, like many others across India and Nepal, was set up by the Tibetan community in exile. The goal was not just to give us an education, but to make sure we never forgot who we were. We studied English and science, but we also learned Tibetan language, history, and values—things that kept our identity alive even when we were far from home.

I grew up in a group home with other children who had taken the same journey I had. We called our caregivers Ama and Aba—mother and father. They weren’t our parents by blood, but they gave us love and a sense of belonging. That small family, along with our teachers and friends, helped us grow up strong, even though many of us had arrived in India with nothing.

Speaking Up, Giving Back

“When we lift others, we lift ourselves.”

My journey of speaking up began at the Tibetan Homes School in Mussoorie, where I started a group with friends called the Students General Improvement Group (SGIG). We weren’t doing anything big—we just wanted to help each other do better, study harder, and show up for one another. It was about believing that even as students, we could shape the way we lived and learned.

That same spirit came with me to college in Bengaluru. There, I tried to bring Tibetan students together, to make sure we didn’t lose touch with our roots. We organized events, held gatherings, and reminded each other why it mattered to stay connected to our language and our community—even when the city around us felt like a different world.

Later, while working with the Tibetan government-in-exile, I helped lead a project to build our first digital demographic system. The goal was simple: help us stay connected as a people scattered around the world. What started as a technical task became something much more—it was a way of telling every Tibetan: “You matter. We see you.” That work led to a deeply meaningful moment: a personal audience with His Holiness the Dalai Lama.

“We are a small nation, but our voice is strong when we speak for each other.”

Finding My Way Forward

“The path may be long, but every step matters.”

During my time in Bengaluru, I started something new—something that felt both risky and necessary. I founded TibetanJournal.com, an online platform for Tibetan news and voices. I wanted to make sure the world heard about our struggles, our achievements, our questions. We didn’t have to wait for others to tell our stories—we could tell them ourselves.

Later, I was honored to receive the Tibetan Scholarship Program from the U.S. State Department. That brought me to Buffalo, New York, where I studied Computer Science and Engineering. I never thought I’d be here—so far from home, and yet still carrying it with me. In 2023, I received the Patricia Eberlein Best Thesis Award for my work, and it gave me strength to keep going.

Now, as a Presidential Fellow pursuing a Ph.D. in education, I focus on helping displaced and marginalized students—especially Tibetan youth—learn computing in ways that honor their culture and stories. For me, code is not just technology. It’s a new language for an old dream: to help our people stay strong, seen, and connected.

“Exile gave me purpose. Learning gave me tools. Together, they gave me direction.”
